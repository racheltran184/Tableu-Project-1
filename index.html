Victoria counts crashes.
Its deaths are somewhere else.

Thirteen years of Victorian police crash records: 184,699 crashes and 3,318 deaths. Crashes peak in Melbourne's Friday rush. Deaths concentrate somewhere else: on regional highways, at night, in old cars and among people without a seatbelt. I traced each death to its road, hour and crash type, then set eleven actions against those places, each with an owner, a trigger and a measure.

01 · The brief

Halve road deaths by 2030.
Where do we start?

Victoria's Road Safety Strategy 2021–2030 sets the goal: halve road deaths by 2030 and cut serious injuries. A dashboard of crash counts shows where traffic is. It doesn't say where people die, so it can't say where a barrier, a speed limit or a patrol hour saves a life.

This study asks each question in deaths and serious injuries, and takes every answer down to a road, an hour, a crash type and a person, so each action has a place to start.

Who asks what

Road safety leadershipAre deaths on track to halve by 2030, and what is driving the gap?
Transport planningWhich roads should get barriers, line marking or a lower limit first?
PoliceWhich hours and roads should enforcement cover to prevent the most deaths?
Accident insurerWhich road users and vehicles need their own program?

How to read the numbers

  • MeasureDeaths and serious injuries, not crash counts. The 2030 target is about deaths, and most crashes injure without killing.
  • Rates"Deaths per 100 crashes" compares how lethal a crash is by place, hour or type. The data has no traffic volumes or population, so it is not risk per kilometre driven.
  • ScopePolice-reported crashes with an injury. Serious injuries for 2024 fall by a fifth while crashes hold steady; 2024 serious-injury figures are treated as provisional.

03 · What the data says

Eight findings,
each pointing to a place.

Data window

Police-reported injury crashes from 1 January 2012 to 31 December 2024: 184,699 crashes, 3,318 deaths and 76,174 people seriously injured.

Measures

Deaths per 100 crashes; share of deaths against share of crashes; deaths a year against a straight line to half the 2018–20 average by 2030.

Read with care

Rates are per crash, not per kilometre driven. Each finding names what the data can't separate.
01

Deaths are rising, away from the 2030 line.

Crashes have held between 14,000 and 14,900 a year since 2021. Deaths have not: 211 in 2020, then 295 in 2023, the most in 13 years, and 284 in 2024.

From the 2018–20 average of 230 deaths, a straight line to half reaches 115 in 2030. It sits at 184 in 2024. Victoria finished 2024 100 deaths above it, and the line falls another 11.5 deaths every year.

So whatA crash count can fall while deaths rise, because most crashes don't kill: rear-end crashes are 18% of crashes and 5% of deaths. The measure that matches the target is deaths and serious injuries against the line. In 2025 the line is at 173.
Read the code: deaths against the line 
# Deaths against a straight line to half the 2018-20 average by 2030
yr = harm(crash, 'year')
base = yr.loc[2018:2020, 'deaths'].mean()


def glide(y):
    return base - (base / 2) * (y - 2020) / 10


out['glide'] = {'base': base, 'target2030': base / 2, 'rows': [
    {'y': int(y), 'deaths': int(r.deaths), 'serious': int(r.serious), 'crashes': int(r.crashes),
     'path': glide(y) if y >= 2020 else None} for y, r in yr.iterrows()]}
out['glide']['next'] = [{'y': y, 'path': glide(y)} for y in range(2025, 2031)]

02

Most crashes don't kill. Leaving the lane does.

Cars that ran off the road or lost control make up 23% of crashes and 40% of deaths. Head-on crashes are 3% of crashes and 16% of deaths: 8.7 deaths per 100 crashes, nearly five times the average of 1.8.

Together these lane departures account for 56% of all deaths; in regional Victoria, 69%. Rear-end crashes are the opposite: 18% of crashes, 5% of deaths. In Melbourne the next biggest killer is a pedestrian being hit (23% of Melbourne deaths).

So whatBarriers, centre lines, edge lines and speed on high-speed roads are aimed at the crash type behind most deaths. Intersection and rear-end programs reduce crash counts and touch a much smaller share of deaths.
Read the code: one crash type per crash 
# One crash type per crash, from the Definitions for Classifying Accidents (DCA)
dca = crash['DCA_CODE_DESCRIPTION'].str.upper()
crash['lane_departure'] = (crash['RUN_OFFROAD'].eq('Yes')
                           | dca.str.contains('OFF CARRIAGEWAY|OFF RIGHT BEND|OFF LEFT BEND|OUT OF CONTROL|OFF END OF ROAD'))
crash['mechanism'] = np.select(
    [dca.str.contains(r'\bPED') | crash['ACCIDENT_TYPE'].eq('Struck Pedestrian'),
     dca.str.contains('HEAD ON'),
     crash['lane_departure'],
     dca.str.contains('CROSS TRAFFIC|RIGHT THROUGH|RIGHT NEAR|LEFT NEAR|RIGHT FAR|LEFT FAR|INTERSECTION'),
     dca.str.contains('REAR END')],
    ['Pedestrian struck', 'Head-on', 'Run-off-road / loss of control', 'Intersection', 'Rear-end'],
    default='Other')
crash.loc[crash['mechanism'].isin(['Pedestrian struck', 'Head-on']), 'lane_departure'] = \
    crash['mechanism'].eq('Head-on')          # head-ons count as lane departure; pedestrian crashes don't


def harm(df, by):
    g = df.groupby(by).agg(crashes=('ACCIDENT_NO', 'size'), deaths=('FATALITY', 'sum'),
                           serious=('SERIOUSINJURY', 'sum'))
    g['deaths_per_100'] = 100 * g['deaths'] / g['crashes']
    g['share_crashes'] = 100 * g['crashes'] / len(df)
    g['share_deaths'] = 100 * g['deaths'] / df['FATALITY'].sum()
    return g


out = {}
T = {'crashes': len(crash), 'deaths': int(crash.FATALITY.sum()), 'serious': int(crash.SERIOUSINJURY.sum()),
     'first': str(crash.ACCIDENT_DATE.min()), 'years': [int(crash.year.min()), int(crash.year.max())]}
T['deaths_per_100'] = 100 * T['deaths'] / T['crashes']
out['totals'] = T

m = harm(crash, 'mechanism').sort_values('deaths', ascending=False)
out['mech'] = [{'k': k, **{c: float(v) for c, v in r.items()}} for k, r in m.iterrows()]
for reg in ['Metro', 'Country']:
    s = crash[crash.region == reg].groupby('mechanism').FATALITY.sum()
    out['mech_' + reg.lower()] = {k: float(100 * v / s.sum()) for k, v in s.items()}

03

The busiest hour is not the deadliest.

Crashes peak from 3 to 6 pm, about 15,000 in each of those hours over 13 years; Friday from 3 to 7 pm alone has 9,458. That traffic kills 1.4 people per 100 crashes: half of Friday's afternoon crashes are at intersections or rear-ends.

From 10 pm to 6 am the roads are quiet, with 10% of crashes and 20% of deaths. Between midnight and 4 am, 4.2 to 4.7 people die per 100 crashes, about 2.5 times the average. Friday has the most crashes; Saturday has the most deaths (542), and Sunday the highest rate. Of 677 night deaths, 386 followed a car leaving the road.

So whatPatrol hours set by crash volume land on the Friday rush. Deaths call for nights and weekends, on the roads where cars leave their lane.
Read the code: hours and weekdays 
h = harm(crash, 'hour')
out['hours'] = [{'h': int(k), 'crashes': int(r.crashes), 'deaths': int(r.deaths),
                 'dp100': float(r.deaths_per_100)} for k, r in h.iterrows()]
night = crash['hour'].ge(22) | crash['hour'].le(5)
out['night'] = {'crashes': int(night.sum()), 'deaths': int(crash.loc[night, 'FATALITY'].sum())}
dow = harm(crash, 'DAY_OF_WEEK')
out['dow'] = [{'d': k, 'crashes': int(r.crashes), 'deaths': int(r.deaths), 'dp100': float(r.deaths_per_100)}
              for k, r in dow.iterrows()]
fri_pm = crash['DAY_OF_WEEK'].eq('Friday') & crash['hour'].between(15, 18)
out['fri_pm'] = {'crashes': int(fri_pm.sum()), 'deaths': int(crash.loc[fri_pm, 'FATALITY'].sum()),
                 'mix': crash.loc[fri_pm, 'mechanism'].value_counts(normalize=True).mul(100).to_dict()}

04

A quarter of deaths: one road type, one crash type.

Regional Victoria has 28% of crashes and 55% of deaths. Its 100–110 km/h roads have 10% of crashes and 39% of deaths. On those roads, three in four deaths follow a vehicle leaving its lane.

Where that road is undivided, 9,998 crashes (5.4%) produced 785 deaths (24%): 7.9 per 100 crashes, more than four times the average. By speed zone, 100 km/h roads see 5.4 deaths per 100 crashes, 80 km/h roads 2.0 and 60 km/h roads 1.1.

So whatThis is a specific, treatable problem: a vehicle drifting across the centre line or off the edge of an undivided high-speed road. Flexible barriers, wide centre lines and audio-tactile line marking are built for that crash.
Read the code: narrowing the deaths 
# Narrow all deaths down to one road type and one crash type
steps = [('All crashes', crash.index == crash.index),
         ('Regional Victoria', crash.region.eq('Country')),
         ('… on 100–110 km/h roads', crash.region.eq('Country') & crash.high_speed),
         ('… where the vehicle left its lane', crash.region.eq('Country') & crash.high_speed & crash.lane_departure),
         ('… on an undivided road', crash.region.eq('Country') & crash.high_speed & crash.lane_departure
          & crash.DIVIDED.eq('Undivided'))]
out['funnel'] = [{'k': k, 'crashes': int(f.sum()), 'deaths': int(crash.loc[f, 'FATALITY'].sum()),
                  'serious': int(crash.loc[f, 'SERIOUSINJURY'].sum())} for k, f in steps]
z = harm(crash, ['region', 'high_speed'])

05

Twelve highways carry a quarter of regional deaths.

The regional sections of 12 roads account for 23% of regional deaths on 11% of regional crashes. The Princes Highway has the most deaths (80). The Murray Valley, Western, Northern, Goulburn Valley, Glenelg and Hamilton highways kill 9 to 11 people per 100 crashes, five to six times the state average.

The councils follow the same roads. Among regional councils with 500 or more crashes, the highest death rates are Moira (7.4 per 100), Corangamite (7.1), Strathbogie (6.8) and Hepburn (6.8). Moira, Campaspe and Greater Shepparton sit on the Murray Valley, Northern and Goulburn Valley highways.

So whatThat gives a treatment list rather than a statewide program: start where both deaths and the death rate are high. The data names the road, not the kilometre, so each corridor still needs a section survey before works.
Read the code: ranking regional roads

               for k, r in lga.iterrows()]
metro = harm(crash[crash.region.eq('Metro') & crash.road.ne('')], 'road').sort_values('deaths', ascending=False)

                      for k, r in metro.head(6).iterrows()]

06

The rise came from two kinds of road.

Deaths averaged 230 a year in 2018–20 and 290 a year in 2023–24. Almost all of the rise came from two places: Melbourne roads of 90 km/h or less (+32 a year) and regional 100–110 km/h roads (+29).

In Melbourne, the growth is lane departure (20 to 35 a year) and intersections (17 to 25). Roads of 90 km/h or less carry 86% of Melbourne's deaths, three in four of them on 60–80 km/h arterials. By road user, motorcyclist deaths rose from 37 to 55 a year, the steepest rise relative to its size. Rear-end deaths fell.

So whatOne statewide campaign won't close the gap. It takes two road programs, regional highways and Melbourne arterials, plus a program of its own for riders.
Read the code: before and after 
# Average deaths a year: 2018-20 against 2023-24
crash['place'] = crash.region + np.where(crash.high_speed, ' · 100–110 km/h', ' · up to 90 km/h')
person = person.merge(crash[['ACCIDENT_NO', 'region', 'high_speed', 'year', 'mechanism', 'hour']], on='ACCIDENT_NO')
person['died'] = person.INJ_LEVEL_DESC.eq('Fatality')
person['ksi'] = person.INJ_LEVEL_DESC.isin(['Fatality', 'Serious injury'])


def change(df, col, val):
    before = df[df.year.between(2018, 2020)].groupby(col)[val].sum() / 3
    after = df[df.year.between(2023, 2024)].groupby(col)[val].sum() / 2
    t = pd.DataFrame({'before': before, 'after': after}).fillna(0)
    t['delta'] = t.after - t.before
    return t.sort_values('delta', ascending=False)


rise = {'place': change(crash, 'place', 'FATALITY'), 'mech': change(crash, 'mechanism', 'FATALITY'),
        'user': change(person[person.ROAD_USER_TYPE_DESC.isin(
            ['Drivers', 'Passengers', 'Motorcyclists', 'Pedestrians', 'Bicyclists'])], 'ROAD_USER_TYPE_DESC', 'died')}
out['rise'] = {g: [{'k': k, 'before': float(r.before), 'after': float(r.after), 'delta': float(r.delta)}
                   for k, r in t.iterrows()] for g, t in rise.items()}
mm = crash[crash.region.eq('Metro') & ~crash.high_speed]
out['rise_metro_mech'] = [{'k': k, **{c: float(v) for c, v in r.items()}} for k, r in change(mm, 'mechanism', 'FATALITY').iterrows()]
out['metro_low_share'] = float(100 * mm.FATALITY.sum() / crash[crash.region.eq('Metro')].FATALITY.sum())

07

The people most likely to die in a crash.

Without a seatbelt, 4.6% of occupants in a crash died, against 0.5% with one. That is 334 deaths, 16% of occupant deaths, and a floor: another 24% of occupant deaths have the belt recorded as unknown. Unbelted deaths are 85% male, 68% regional and four in five are lane departures; at night they are 25% of occupant deaths, against 13% by day.

On foot, 2.9% of people hit died; aged 70 and over, 6.7% (169 deaths), and 72% of pedestrian deaths are in Melbourne. Motorcyclists are 6% of people in crashes and 17% of deaths. Men are 72% of everyone killed.

So whatEach group needs its own lever, placed where it dies: belt checks on regional roads at night, crossings and lower speeds where older people walk, and rider protection on the roads where riders leave the lane.
Read the code: who dies

08

Old cars turn crashes into deaths.

For every 1,000 drivers in a crash, 2.9 died in a car under 5 years old and 13.0 in a car 20 or more years old. Cars 15 or more years old are 25% of drivers in crashes and 44% of driver deaths.

The gap holds where it could have been explained away. On the same roads: regional 100–110 km/h, 28 rising to 54 per 1,000; Melbourne roads of 90 km/h or less, 1.0 to 5.3. For drivers of the same age: 30 to 39, 1.6 to 11.3. With a seatbelt on: regional 100–110 km/h, 27 to 43. Old cars are also more common where crashes are deadlier: 32% of regional crash-involved cars are 15 or more years old, against 22% in Melbourne.

So whatReplacing old cars saves lives in crashes that roads and patrols don't prevent. Regional drivers carry the most risk and the oldest fleet, so a trade-up program should start there.
Read the code: car age against driver deaths 
# Driver deaths per 1,000 drivers in a crash, by the age of their car
LIGHT = ['Car', 'Station Wagon', 'Utility', 'Panel Van', 'Taxi',
         'Light Commercial Vehicle (Rigid) <= 4.5 Tonnes GVM']
v = vehicle.merge(crash[['ACCIDENT_NO', 'year', 'region', 'high_speed']], on='ACCIDENT_NO')
made = pd.to_numeric(v.VEHICLE_YEAR_MANUF, errors='coerce').where(lambda s: s.between(1950, 2025))
v['age'] = v.year - made
drivers = person[person.ROAD_USER_TYPE_DESC.eq('Drivers')][['ACCIDENT_NO', 'VEHICLE_ID', 'died', 'HELMET_BELT_WORN', 'AGE_GROUP']]
cars = v[v.VEHICLE_TYPE_DESC.isin(LIGHT) & v.age.between(-1, 60)].merge(drivers, on=['ACCIDENT_NO', 'VEHICLE_ID'])
cars['band'] = pd.cut(cars.age, [-2, 4, 9, 14, 19, 60], labels=['0–4 years', '5–9 years', '10–14 years', '15–19 years', '20+ years'])
cars['segment'] = cars.region + np.where(cars.high_speed, ' · 100–110 km/h', ' · up to 90 km/h')
va = cars.groupby('band', observed=True).died.agg(['size', 'sum'])
seg = cars.pivot_table(index='segment', columns='band', values='died', aggfunc='mean', observed=False) * 1000
belted = cars[cars.HELMET_BELT_WORN.eq('Seatbelt Worn')]
segb = belted.pivot_table(index='segment', columns='band', values='died', aggfunc='mean', observed=False) * 1000
out['vehage'] = [{'k': str(k), 'n': int(r['size']), 'd': int(r['sum']), 'v': float(1000 * r['sum'] / r['size'])}
                 for k, r in va.iterrows()]
out['vehage_seg'] = {s: [float(x) for x in seg.loc[s]] for s in seg.index}
out['vehage_seg_belted'] = {s: [float(x) for x in segb.loc[s]] for s in segb.index}
by_driver_age = cars.pivot_table(index='AGE_GROUP', columns='band', values='died', aggfunc='mean', observed=False) * 1000

04 · Actions

Each action aimed
at where people die.

Every action names the finding it answers, where it starts and how it is measured, in deaths and serious injuries. The sizes are counts from 2012–2024, not forecasts of lives saved.

Transport planning owns the roads. Treat lane departure on regional highways, slow it where works are years away, and fix Melbourne's arterials.

01

Treat lane departure on 12 highways

Do

Flexible barriers on the centre and shoulders, wide centre lines and audio-tactile line marking on undivided 100–110 km/h sections. Start with the Murray Valley, Northern, Goulburn Valley and Western highways.

Why

Lane departures on undivided regional highways: 785 deaths (24%) from 5.4% of crashes. The 12 roads carry 23% of regional deaths.

Rule

Rank sections by lane-departure deaths and serious injuries over five years; works go to the top of the list first.

TrackLane-departure deaths and serious injuries, treated against untreated sections
02

Lower the limit where works are years away

Do

Review 100 km/h undivided sections in the top quarter for lane-departure harm, where no barrier is funded within two years, for 80 km/h.

Why

100 km/h roads see 5.4 deaths per 100 crashes against 2.0 on 80 km/h roads. Road types differ, so each change is measured before and after.

Rule

Keep the lower limit where deaths and serious injuries fall over 24 months; restore it once a barrier is built.

TrackMean speed and serious crashes per section, before and after
03

Fix Melbourne's 60–80 km/h arterials

Do

Raised platforms, roundabouts and protected right turns at the worst intersections, and roadside protection, starting on the Nepean Highway, Ballarat Road, Ferntree Gully Road and Stud Road.

Why

Melbourne deaths on roads of 90 km/h or less rose from 88 to 120 a year: lane departures 20 to 35, intersections 17 to 25.

TrackDeaths and serious injuries at treated intersections and lengths

Police own the hours. Put enforcement where deaths happen, not where crashes are counted.

04

Move patrol hours to nights and weekends

Do

Shift drug, alcohol and speed operations to 10 pm–6 am and weekends, on the corridors in action 01.

Why

Nights hold 10% of crashes and 20% of deaths; Saturday has the most deaths. 386 of 677 night deaths followed a car leaving the road.

Rule

Keep the shift where night deaths per 100 crashes fall within 12 months.

TrackNight deaths per 100 crashes, regional (4.2–4.7 midnight to 4 am)
05

Check seatbelts on regional roads at night

Do

Add belt checks to every night and weekend operation on regional 100–110 km/h roads, with a message aimed at men.

Why

Unbelted occupants die at 9 times the belted rate. Unbelted deaths are 68% regional and 85% male, and 25% of occupant deaths at night.

TrackUnbelted share of occupant deaths (16% now, plus 24% unknown)
06

Record the belt at every death

Do

Make belt status a required field for every occupant killed or seriously injured, confirmed with the coroner's or hospital record.

Why

504 occupant deaths (24%) have the belt recorded as unknown, so the unbelted toll can't be measured.

TrackOccupant deaths with belt status unknown (target: under 5%)

The accident insurer and councils own the programs. One lever for each group, aimed where that group dies.

07

A rider program for lane departure

Do

Fit barrier underrun protection for riders on the corridor sections where riders crash, and fund rider training on cornering and intersections.

Why

Motorcyclist deaths rose from 37 to 55 a year. 221 of 549 rider deaths followed leaving the road; 129 were at intersections.

TrackRider deaths a year, by crash type
08

Slow traffic where older people walk

Do

40 km/h limits and longer crossing times in Melbourne shopping strips and near aged-care and health services.

Why

6.7% of pedestrians aged 70+ who are hit die; 169 deaths, a third of all pedestrian deaths. 72% of pedestrian deaths are in Melbourne.

TrackPedestrian deaths and serious injuries, aged 70+
09

Trade up old cars, regional first

Do

A subsidy or low-rate loan to replace cars 15+ years old with a five-star safety-rated used car, offered first in councils on the corridor list.

Why

Cars 15+ years old are 25% of drivers in crashes and 44% of driver deaths; the gap holds on the same roads, for the same driver ages and with a belt on.

TrackShare of crash-involved cars aged 15+ (32% regional now)

Road safety leadership owns the scorecard. Measure what the target measures, and add the exposure the data is missing.

10

Report deaths against the line, every quarter

Do

A quarterly scorecard of deaths and serious injuries against the line to half by 2030, split by regional highways, Melbourne arterials, riders and pedestrians.

Why

2024 ended 100 deaths above the line while crashes held flat. A crash count can't show that gap.

Rule

Any group above its share of the line for two quarters gets a program review.

TrackDeaths against the line (173 in 2025, 115 in 2030)
11

Add exposure: traffic, length, population

Do

Join traffic counts and section lengths to the crash table, and council population by year, then rank roads per 100 million kilometres driven.

Why

Deaths per 100 crashes show how lethal a crash is, not how risky a road is to drive. Works are funded by section, so the ranking needs length and volume.

Treat the roads that kill,

Closing a gap of 100 deaths takes programs sized to where deaths are: regional highways, Melbourne arterials, the hours and people at most risk and a scorecard in deaths. Lane departures on undivided regional highways alone were 785 deaths over 13 years, about 60 a year.
